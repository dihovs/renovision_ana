**The gap we're filling:** every single result stops at the pipe. They tell you
how to stop it freezing, how to thaw it, and who to call for the plumbing. Not
one of them covers what happens *after* it bursts — the water in the wall
cavity, the ceiling below, the finished basement, and the insurance file that
starts the moment it lets go.

That is exactly the split the ceiling page already exploits: the plumber's job
ends where ours begins. Here it is even cleaner, because two of the top results
are French sites describing a French winter and a French claim process.

## Our angle

Two winter calls, one rebuild. A frozen pipe and a failing water heater produce
the same wet drywall and the same soaked subfloor, but they are opposite
insurance stories — one is sudden and accidental, the other has usually been
weeping quietly for months. That distinction decides the claim, and nobody in
this SERP mentions it because none of them is looking at the file.

We do not compete on how to thaw a pipe. CAA-Québec does that well and we cite
them for it. We compete on the hour after.

## Facts to cite

| Claim | Source |

| Signs of a frozen pipe: reduced or absent flow, unusual noises in the piping, a section abnormally cold | CAA-Québec |
| Thaw with a hair dryer, tap open, moving slowly toward the frozen section; heating blankets, small radiators or heat lamps also acceptable | CAA-Québec |
| Never a blowtorch or propane torch — damages plastic pipe and soldered joints, and can start a fire | CAA-Québec |
| Pipes in above-grade exterior walls and unheated spaces are the most vulnerable; crawl spaces less so, being warmed from above | CAA-Québec |
